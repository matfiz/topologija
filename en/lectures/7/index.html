Lecture 7

Smooth manifolds

What this lecture does

A smooth manifold has local coordinates whose changes are smooth. This allows derivatives to be compared between coordinate systems. We construct coordinates on spheres and projective spaces, then prove that regular constraints define manifolds using the implicit function theorem. The space of pure qubit states gives a concrete example: a sphere described by two complex coordinate charts.

7.1 Charts and smooth structures

Definition 7.1 (Topological manifold). A topological nnn-manifold is a Hausdorff, second-countable space MMM locally homeomorphic to open subsets of Rn\R^nRn. Here second-countable means that its topology has a countable basis.

Definition 7.6 (Smooth structure). A smooth structure is a maximal smooth atlas, containing every chart compatible with it.

Definition 7.7 (Smooth manifold). A smooth manifold is a topological manifold with a smooth structure.

A covering atlas specifies which coordinate descriptions are compatible. Its maximal extension includes every chart compatible with that choice.

Any smooth atlas determines a unique smooth structure. To prove this, adjoin every chart compatible with the given atlas. For two added charts ψ\psiψ and θ\thetaθ and a point in their overlap, choose an original chart φ\varphiφ containing that point. Locally the transition is θ∘ψ−1=(θ∘φ−1)∘(φ∘ψ−1).\theta\circ\psi^{-1} =(\theta\circ\varphi^{-1})\circ(\varphi\circ\psi^{-1}).θ∘ψ−1=(θ∘φ−1)∘(φ∘ψ−1). Both factors are smooth. Reversing the roles gives a smooth inverse. Thus the enlarged collection is an atlas. Any chart compatible with the enlarged collection was compatible with the original one and has already been added. This proves maximality. Every chart of any maximal atlas containing the original atlas belongs to this enlarged collection. That maximal atlas must therefore equal the enlarged collection, proving uniqueness.

The smooth structure is extra data. On the topological line, the global charts x(t)=tx(t)=tx(t)=t and y(t)=t3y(t)=t^3y(t)=t3 individually define smooth structures. Their transition y∘x−1(s)=s3y\circ x^{-1}(s)=s^3y∘x−1(s)=s3 is smooth, but its inverse is not differentiable at zero. The function g(t)=tg(t)=tg(t)=t has expression g∘x−1(s)=sg\circ x^{-1}(s)=sg∘x−1(s)=s in the first structure and g∘y−1(s)=s3g\circ y^{-1}(s)=\sqrt[3]{s}g∘y−1(s)=3s​ in the second. Thus the two structures give different sets of smooth real-valued functions. We compare these structures by a smooth map in §7.3.

Local Euclidean coordinates alone do not imply Hausdorffness. Identify two copies of R\RR at every nonzero coordinate, leaving two origins. Each origin has an interval chart, but any two neighbourhoods of the origins intersect. The sequence with coordinates 1/k1/k1/k, for positive integers kkk, converges to both origins. Hausdorffness excludes this failure of uniqueness of limits. The drawing separates the two copies vertically only to distinguish their origins.

An uncountable disjoint union of lines is locally Euclidean and Hausdorff. It is not second-countable: each component would need a different nonempty basis element contained in it. Second countability supplies the countable chart constructions used for partitions of unity and integration in Lecture 12.

A smooth invertible transition also fixes the dimension. By the chain rule, its derivative has a two-sided inverse, so its source and target have the same dimension.

Definition 7.20 (Immersion). A smooth map is an immersion if its coordinate Jacobian is injective at every point.

Summary

A smooth structure is specified by charts with smooth transitions. Smooth maps are tested in these charts, and the Euclidean chain rule makes the test independent of coordinates. The implicit function theorem turns independent smooth constraints into embedded submanifolds. The qubit state space has the two projective charts zzz and w=1/zw=1/zw=1/z, and is diffeomorphic to the Bloch sphere.
