Lecture 28

The index on the sphere

What this lecture does

Lecture 22 associated an integer with the flux through a sphere. We now use that integer to count quantum zero modes of a Dirac operator. The spin connection changes the line bundle in which one chiral component lives. On the round sphere this turns the count into a polynomial calculation. The cited index theorem explains why the difference of the two chiral counts is topological.

28.1 Spinors and the canonical line on the sphere

Use the unit round sphere with its outward orientation. Identify it with CP1\CP1CP1 using Lecture 20’s complex coordinates zzz and w=1/zw=1/zw=1/z. The complex orientation agrees with this outward orientation. A spinor field is a section of a spinor bundle, the geometric bundle on which the Dirac equation acts. Its construction is a cited input here (Lawson–Michelsohn, Chapter II and Appendix D). On an oriented surface it has two complex line components S+S^+S+ and S−S^-S−. Their labels specify chirality, a grading with eigenvalues +1+1+1 and −1-1−1. Chirality here is a spinor grading, distinct from even and odd differential-form degree.

The canonical bundle KS2\canonicalbundleKS2​ is the line of holomorphic cotangent vectors. Its local frames are dz\dd zdz and dw\dd wdw. Since dw=−z−2dz\dd w=-z^{-2}\dd zdw=−z−2dz, absorbing the constant minus sign into one frame gives KS2≅O(−2)\canonicalbundle\cong\degreeline{-2}KS2​≅O(−2) in Lecture 22’s frame convention. We choose the positive spinor line to be its square root: S+=KS21/2≅O(−1),(KS21/2)⊗2≅KS2.S^+=\canonicalbundle^{1/2}\cong\degreeline{-1},\qquad (\canonicalbundle^{1/2})^{\otimes2}\cong\canonicalbundle.S+=KS21/2​≅O(−1),(KS21/2​)⊗2≅KS2​. This square-root choice is part of the cited spinor identification. We do not construct spin structures in this lecture.

Recall that Ln=O(n)L_n=\degreeline nLn​=O(n) is the monopole line of Chern number n∈Zn\in\Zn∈Z. Twisting means taking the tensor product with this charged line. Put En=S+⊗Ln≅O(n−1)E_n=S^+\otimes L_n\cong\degreeline{n-1}En​=S+⊗Ln​≅O(n−1). The full twisted spinor bundle has the cited description S⊗Ln≅(Λ0,0⊕Λ0,1)⊗En.S\otimes L_n\cong \bigl(\Lambda^{0,0}\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}\bigr)\otimes E_n.S⊗Ln​≅(Λ0,0⊕Λ0,1)⊗En​. Here a (0,0)(0,0)(0,0)-form is a function and a (0,1)(0,1)(0,1)-form is locally a multiple of dzˉ\dd\bar zdzˉ. The positive part is EnE_nEn​. The negative part is Λ0,1⊗En\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes E_nΛ0,1⊗En​. The figure records the twist that will change the polynomial count.

These identifications are stated in Lawson–Michelsohn, Appendix D, equation (D.16), p. 396. Walpuski, Propositions 70.3 and 70.6, pp. 76–78, give the form decomposition and square-root construction explicitly.

28.2 The Dirac operator and its index

Write Ω0,k(En)\Omega^{0,k}(E_n)Ω0,k(En​) for the smooth EnE_nEn​-valued (0,k)(0,k)(0,k)-forms. The Dolbeault operator ∂ˉA\dbar_A∂ˉA​ is the (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) part of the covariant derivative. In a holomorphic frame it sends a coefficient fff to (∂zˉf)dzˉ(\partial_{\bar z}f)\dd\bar z(∂zˉ​f)dzˉ, as in Lecture 22. Its adjoint ∂ˉA∗\dbar_A^*∂ˉA∗​ is defined by the Hermitian L2L^2L2 inner products from the bundle metric and sphere area. The first slot of these inner products is antilinear. There are no (0,2)(0,2)(0,2)-forms on a complex curve.

Proposition 28.1 (The sphere Dirac operatorsketched). With the above chirality, the twisted Dirac operator is D=2(∂ˉA+∂ˉA∗)=(0D−D+0),D+=2∂ˉA,D−=(D+)∗=2∂ˉA∗.\Dirac=\sqrt2(\dbar_A+\dbar_A^*) =\begin{pmatrix}0&\Diracminus\\\Diracplus&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Diracplus=\sqrt2\dbar_A,\qquad \Diracminus=(\Diracplus)^*=\sqrt2\dbar_A^*.D=2​(∂ˉA​+∂ˉA∗​)=(0D+​D−0​),D+=2​∂ˉA​,D−=(D+)∗=2​∂ˉA∗​. Explanation. The cited spinor identification turns Clifford multiplication into exterior multiplication by an antiholomorphic covector and its adjoint contraction. Contracting the spin connection and monopole connection with it gives the displayed operator. This identification, including its 2\sqrt22​ normalization, is Walpuski’s Proposition 70.3. For a flat metric dx2+dy2\dd x^2+\dd y^2dx2+dy2, the unit antiholomorphic coframe is dzˉ/2\dd\bar z/\sqrt2dzˉ/2​. Thus the positive block has coefficient 2∂zˉ2\partial_{\bar z}2∂zˉ​ and the negative block has coefficient −2∂z-2\partial_z−2∂z​. Their product is −4∂z∂zˉ=−∂x2−∂y2-4\partial_z\partial_{\bar z}=-\partial_x^2-\partial_y^2−4∂z​∂zˉ​=−∂x2​−∂y2​, the nonnegative scalar Laplacian of Lecture 15. To compare with the familiar flat Dirac equation, let σx\sigma_xσx​ and σy\sigma_yσy​ be the Pauli matrices. The corresponding self-adjoint spatial operator is −i(σx∂x+σy∂y)-\ii(\sigma_x\partial_x+\sigma_y\partial_y)−i(σx​∂x​+σy​∂y​). Multiplying its negative component by i\iii gives the displayed chiral blocks. This verifies the local normalization and recalls the flat operator, not the global spinor construction. 

A zero mode is a smooth spinor annihilated by D\DiracD. We use its self-adjoint realization on the closed sphere with the first-order Sobolev domain defined next. This is supplied by cited elliptic spectral theory (Lawson–Michelsohn, Theorem 5.8, pp. 196–197), with the domain identified by the elliptic estimate in Theorem 5.2. The first-order Sobolev domain consists of square-integrable sections whose first derivatives, understood by integration by parts, are also square-integrable in local charts. Its chiral block is Fredholm: between this domain and L2L^2L2 it has closed range and finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel. The cokernel is the target modulo the range, and is identified with ker⁡D−\ker\DiracminuskerD− by the adjoint. These analytic facts follow from cited elliptic theory (Lawson–Michelsohn, Chapter III, Theorem 5.2, pp. 193–194). The index is the signed difference ind⁡D+=dim⁡ker⁡D+−dim⁡ker⁡D−.\operatorname{ind}\Diracplus=\dim\ker\Diracplus-\dim\ker\Diracminus.indD+=dimkerD+−dimkerD−. Both dimensions are over C\CC. An index of zero alone does not say that either kernel vanishes.

Along a norm-continuous path of Fredholm chiral blocks from the fixed Sobolev domain to L2L^2L2, the index is constant by the cited Fredholm stability theorem (Lawson–Michelsohn, Proposition 7.1 and Corollary 7.4, pp. 201–202). Each kernel dimension can change, with equal changes in the two counts. The finite-dimensional model Tt ⁣:C2→CT_t\colon\C^2\to\CTt​:C2→C, given by Tt(a,b)=taT_t(a,b)=taTt​(a,b)=ta, illustrates this: for t≠0t\ne0t=0 its kernel and adjoint kernel have dimensions 111 and 000. At t=0t=0t=0 they have dimensions 222 and 111. Their difference stays one. This is a model for index stability, not a claim that these jumps occur within the special sphere family below. Perturbations that destroy the chiral block structure are outside this statement.

The square D2\Dirac^2D2 preserves chirality. Its positive-energy states pair across the two components: if D−D+u=ϵu\Diracminus\Diracplus u=\epsilon uD−D+u=ϵu with ϵ>0\epsilon>0ϵ>0, then D+u/ϵ\Diracplus u/\sqrt\epsilonD+u/ϵ​ is a normalized state of D+D−\Diracplus\DiracminusD+D− with the same eigenvalue. Its norm is one because ∥D+u∥2=⟨u,D−D+u⟩=ϵ\|\Diracplus u\|^2=\langle u,\Diracminus\Diracplus u\rangle=\epsilon∥D+u∥2=⟨u,D−D+u⟩=ϵ for ∥u∥=1\|u\|=1∥u∥=1. The reverse map is D−/ϵ\Diracminus/\sqrt\epsilonD−/ϵ​. The figure shows why a difference can remain only at zero.

28.4 The surface index theorem

The polynomial count determines the left side of an index theorem for this sphere connection. The right side is the flux integer already computed in Lecture 22.

Theorem 28.5 (Atiyah–Singer on a surfacecited). Let MMM be a closed oriented Riemannian spin surface and LLL a Hermitian line bundle with unitary connection of curvature Fconn\FconnFconn​. Choose positive spinors in the square root of the canonical line for the chosen spin structure. For the corresponding chiral twisted Dirac operator, ind⁡D+=∫Mc1(L)=i2π∫MFconn.\operatorname{ind}\Diracplus=\int_M\chernone(L) =\frac{\ii}{2\pi}\int_M\Fconn.indD+=∫M​c1​(L)=2πi​∫M​Fconn​.

This is the surface specialization of the twisted Dirac index theorem (Nakahara, equations (12.85)–(12.86)). The curvature here is anti-Hermitian, as in Lecture 22. For the sphere monopole, Fconn=−insin⁡θ dθ∧dφ/2\Fconn=-\ii n\sin\theta\,\dd\theta\wedge\dd\varphi/2Fconn​=−insinθdθ∧dφ/2. Its curvature integral gives i(−2πin)/(2π)=n\ii(-2\pi\ii n)/(2\pi)=ni(−2πin)/(2π)=n, agreeing with the kernel count. The theorem applies beyond this symmetric monopole connection. It fixes the difference of the kernels under smooth changes of the metric and connection on the same graded bundle. It does not generally fix either kernel dimension separately. An arbitrary prescribed pair of nonnegative dimensions need not be realizable by such a family.

28.5 The two-dimensional chiral anomaly

Remark 28.6 (Axial charge and indexcited). For this application reinterpret two dimensions as Euclidean spacetime. The spatial-first coordinates are (x,τ)(x,\tau)(x,τ) with orientation dx∧dτ\dd x\wedge\dd\taudx∧dτ and z=x+iτz=x+\ii\tauz=x+iτ. The Wick rotation is t=−iτt=-\ii\taut=−iτ, so the local holomorphic argument becomes z=x−tz=x-tz=x−t. A fixed value of x−tx-tx−t moves toward increasing xxx. Positive Euclidean chirality therefore continues to right movers. This local convention matches the Euclidean grading to the Lorentzian one.

For one massless Dirac fermion let jRj_RjR​ and jLj_LjL​ be the right and left number currents. Define the axial current by j5=jR−jLj_5=j_R-j_Lj5​=jR​−jL​. Let Q5Q_5Q5​ be its spatially integrated charge. With the vector current conserved, the cited integrated anomaly relation is ΔQ5=2ind⁡D+.\Delta Q_5=2\operatorname{ind}\Diracplus.ΔQ5​=2indD+. It concerns the index of the corresponding Euclidean gauge background and assumes no axial charge escapes through the spatial boundary during the transition. It is not inferred by treating the spatial monopole sphere as Lorentzian spacetime. A background of index +1+1+1 gives a change +2+2+2 in this axial convention. The factor two comes from the Dirac fermion’s two chiral contributions to the axial imbalance. Bilal prints the current relation in four dimensions: (3.30) in §3.5, p. 14, has divergence −a-a−a, and (3.41) in §3.7, p. 16, has integrated anomaly function −2-2−2 times the index, provided the Euclidean and Minkowski chiralities are matched. The regulated chirality-trace argument (3.39)–(3.40) is independent of dimension. Nonzero modes cancel in pairs. The zero modes contribute their signed difference. The fermion and antifermion measures supply the factor two. Repeating that argument with the two-dimensional measure gives the stated relation. The two minus signs give its positive factor. Bilal extends the index computation to arbitrary even dimension in (3.44)–(3.47). This is the cited quantum-field-theory input for the two-dimensional remark, rather than a claim that his printed (3.41) is a two-dimensional formula.

Self-check.

For n=0n=0n=0, determine the sphere index and both zero-mode counts. Explain what follows from the index alone.

Answer.

The sphere theorem gives index zero and both kernel dimensions zero. The index alone says only that the two dimensions agree. Their vanishing uses the sphere-specific polynomial and Serre-duality count.

Summary

The positive spinor line on the outward sphere is O(−1)\degreeline{-1}O(−1). Twisting by the monopole line O(n)\degreeline nO(n) gives O(n−1)\degreeline{n-1}O(n−1). The polynomial proof and cited spinor, Hodge and Serre identifications give chiral counts max⁡(n,0)\max(n,0)max(n,0) and max⁡(−n,0)\max(-n,0)max(−n,0). Their difference is nnn, agreeing with the cited surface index theorem and the flux Chern number. For positive nnn, the spinor count is nnn and the spinless lowest Landau-level count is n+1n+1n+1. With the stated spacetime, chirality and boundary conventions, the cited axial anomaly gives ΔQ5=2ind⁡D+\Delta Q_5=2\operatorname{ind}\DiracplusΔQ5​=2indD+. The course’s topological integers thus constrain both classical boundary sectors and quantum zero modes.
