Lecture 28

The index on the sphere

What this lecture does

Lecture 22 associated an integer with the flux through a sphere. We now use that integer to count quantum zero modes of a Dirac operator. The spin connection changes the line bundle in which one chiral component lives. On the round sphere this turns the count into a polynomial calculation. The cited index theorem explains why the difference of the two chiral counts is topological.

28.1 Spinors and the canonical line on the sphere

We use the unit round sphere with its outward orientation. We identify it with CP1\CP1CP1 using Lecture 20’s complex coordinates zzz and w=1/zw=1/zw=1/z. The complex orientation agrees with this outward orientation.

Definition 28.1 (Spinor field). A spinor field is a section of a spinor bundle, the geometric bundle on which the Dirac equation acts.

Its construction is a cited input (Lawson–Michelsohn, Chapter II and Appendix D). On an oriented surface it has two complex line components S+S^+S+ and S−S^-S−.

Definition 28.2 (Chirality). The chirality of a spinor is its grading with eigenvalues +1+1+1 and −1-1−1 on S+S^+S+ and S−S^-S−.

Spinor chirality is distinct from even and odd differential-form degree.

Definition 28.3 (Canonical bundle). The canonical bundle KS2\canonicalbundleKS2​ is the line of holomorphic cotangent vectors.

Its local frames are dz\dd zdz and dw\dd wdw. Since dw=−z−2dz\dd w=-z^{-2}\dd zdw=−z−2dz, absorbing the constant minus sign into one frame gives KS2≅O(−2)\canonicalbundle\cong\degreeline{-2}KS2​≅O(−2) in Lecture 22’s frame convention. We choose the positive spinor line to be its square root: S+=KS21/2≅O(−1),(KS21/2)⊗2≅KS2.S^+=\canonicalbundle^{1/2}\cong\degreeline{-1},\qquad (\canonicalbundle^{1/2})^{\otimes2}\cong\canonicalbundle.S+=KS21/2​≅O(−1),(KS21/2​)⊗2≅KS2​. This square-root choice is part of the cited spinor identification.

We can model these two lines by the spin eigenlines from Lecture 24. At a point n∈S2\mathbf n\in S^2n∈S2, the matrix n⋅σ\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigman⋅σ has eigenvalues +1+1+1 and −1-1−1. The aligned eigenline has Chern number −1-1−1 and models S+S^+S+. The opposite eigenline has Chern number +1+1+1 and models S−S^-S−.

In the northern unit frame, Lecture 24 gives the aligned connection AN=i(1−cos⁡θ)dφ/2\mathcal A_N=\ii(1-\cos\theta)\dd\varphi/2AN​=i(1−cosθ)dφ/2. This is half the canonical-line connection, because the canonical line is the dual of the tangent line of Lecture 22.

The projected eigenline connections give the spin connections in this sphere model. The identification of their Dirac operator with the intrinsic operator remains the cited spinor input. A spin expectation direction alone does not establish that operator identity.

Recall that Ln=O(n)L_n=\degreeline nLn​=O(n) is the monopole line of Chern number n∈Zn\in\Zn∈Z. Twisting means taking the tensor product with this charged line. The positive twisted line is S+⊗Ln≅O(n−1)S^+\otimes L_n\cong\degreeline{n-1}S+⊗Ln​≅O(n−1). The full twisted spinor bundle has the cited description S⊗Ln≅(Λ0,0⊕Λ0,1)⊗O(n−1).S\otimes L_n\cong \bigl(\Lambda^{0,0}\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}\bigr)\otimes \degreeline{n-1}.S⊗Ln​≅(Λ0,0⊕Λ0,1)⊗O(n−1). Here a (0,0)(0,0)(0,0)-form is a function and a (0,1)(0,1)(0,1)-form is locally a multiple of dzˉ\dd\bar zdzˉ. The positive part is O(n−1)\degreeline{n-1}O(n−1). 28.4 The surface index theorem

The square D2\Dirac^2D2 preserves chirality. Its positive-energy states pair across the two components: if D−D+u=ϵu\Diracminus\Diracplus u=\epsilon uD−D+u=ϵu with ϵ>0\epsilon>0ϵ>0, then D+u/ϵ\Diracplus u/\sqrt\epsilonD+u/ϵ​ is a normalized state of D+D−\Diracplus\DiracminusD+D− with the same eigenvalue. Definition 28.14 (First-order Sobolev domain). The first-order Sobolev domain consists of square-integrable sections whose first derivatives, understood by integration by parts, are also square-integrable in local charts.

Definition 28.15 (Fredholm operator). An operator between the first-order Sobolev domain and L2L^2L2 is Fredholm if it has closed range and finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel.

The chiral block is Fredholm by the cited elliptic theory. The cokernel is the target modulo the range, and is identified with ker⁡D−\ker\DiracminuskerD− by the adjoint. These analytic facts follow from cited elliptic theory (Lawson–Michelsohn, Chapter III, Theorem 5.2, pp. 193–194).

Along a norm-continuous path of Fredholm chiral blocks from the fixed Sobolev domain to L2L^2L2, the index is constant by the cited Fredholm stability theorem (Lawson–Michelsohn, Proposition 7.1 and Corollary 7.4, pp. 201–202). Each kernel dimension can change, with equal changes in the two counts.

The finite-dimensional model Tt ⁣:C2→CT_t\colon\C^2\to\CTt​:C2→C, given by Tt(a,b)=taT_t(a,b)=taTt​(a,b)=ta, illustrates this: for t≠0t\ne0t=0 its kernel and adjoint kernel have dimensions 111 and 000. The separate counts in the symmetric sphere family were fixed by the explicit count. Perturbations that destroy the chiral block structure are outside this statement.

Self-check.

State in one sentence what the chiral index is and why we introduced it.

Answer.

The chiral index is the difference of the complex zero-mode dimensions, introduced because positive squared-Dirac eigenvalues pair across chiralities and only the zero modes can leave an imbalance. The surface index theorem identifies that difference with the curvature integer.

28.5 The two-dimensional chiral anomaly (optional)

For this application we reinterpret two dimensions as Euclidean spacetime. Definition 28.16 (Chiral anomaly). An anomaly is a failure after quantization of a classical conservation law. For the massless Dirac field with vector gauge coupling, the chiral anomaly concerns the separate right- and left-moving fermion-number currents.

Physical input.
