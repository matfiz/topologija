Lecture 6

Homology and the Euler characteristic

What this lecture does

Homology distinguishes closed chains from chains that bound higher-dimensional pieces. A decomposition into triangles makes this distinction a calculation with boundary matrices. We compute these matrices for the torus and the projective plane. Their alternating dimensions give the Euler characteristic. On a periodic lattice, the same quotient describes classes of closed strings in the toric code.

6.1 Simplices and their identifications

A kkk-simplex is the convex hull of k+1k+1k+1 affinely independent points, with a chosen ordering of these vertices. The standard simplex is Δk={(t0,…,tk)∈Rk+1:ti≥0, t0+⋯+tk=1}.\Delta^k=\{(t_0,\ldots,t_k)\in\R^{k+1}:t_i\ge0,\ t_0+\cdots+t_k=1\}.Δk={(t0​,…,tk​)∈Rk+1:ti​≥0, t0​+⋯+tk​=1}. Deleting one vertex gives a face, with its inherited order. A point, an interval and a triangle are simplices of dimensions zero, one and two. The order of the vertices orients an edge from the first vertex to the second. It also specifies the signs of higher-dimensional faces.

A Δ\DeltaΔ-complex structure on XXX is a collection of continuous maps σ ⁣:Δk→X\sigma\colon\Delta^k\to Xσ:Δk→X, called its simplices, with the following properties. Each map is injective on the interior of its domain. These interiors have disjoint images and together cover XXX. The restriction to each face is a designated lower-dimensional simplex, using the affine identification preserving the vertex order. The topology is specified by these maps: a subset A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X is closed exactly when σ−1(A)\sigma^{-1}(A)σ−1(A) is closed for every simplex σ\sigmaσ. Thus the space is obtained by attaching the faces in the specified way.

Different vertices of one domain simplex may have the same image in XXX. An edge can therefore become a loop. By contrast, in a simplicial complex each simplex has distinct vertices in the space and any two simplices intersect in a common face or not at all. The torus model below has one vertex and two triangles. It is a Δ\DeltaΔ-complex, not a simplicial complex. Vertex names such as [v0,v1,v2][v_0,v_1,v_2][v0​,v1​,v2​] refer to ordered domain vertices even when some images coincide.

6.2 Chains and the boundary map

Fix a coefficient ring RRR. Our default is R=ZR=\ZR=Z. The alternatives used here are Z2\Z_2Z2​, the field with arithmetic modulo two, and the real field R\RR. Integers form a ring, not a field. A kkk-chain is a finite formal sum of the kkk-simplices with coefficients in RRR. These sums form the chain group Ck(X;R)C_k(X;R)Ck​(X;R). Chains are added and multiplied by coefficients componentwise. Every chain has a unique expression in the simplex generators. When RRR is a field, they form a vector-space basis. Write C−1=0C_{-1}=0C−1​=0.

The boundary map ∂k ⁣:Ck(X;R)→Ck−1(X;R)\partial_k\colon C_k(X;R)\to C_{k-1}(X;R)∂k​:Ck​(X;R)→Ck−1​(X;R) is the linear map defined on a simplex by alternating deletion: ∂k[v0,…,vk]=∑i=0k(−1)i[v0,…,v^i,…,vk](k≥1),∂0=0.\partial_k[v_0,\ldots,v_k] =\sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^i[v_0,\ldots,\widehat v_i,\ldots,v_k] \quad(k\ge1),\qquad \partial_0=0.∂k​[v0​,…,vk​]=i=0∑k​(−1)i[v0​,…,vi​,…,vk​](k≥1),∂0​=0. The hat means that the vertex is omitted. Each face is read as the designated simplex of the complex. This ∂\partial∂ acts on chains; it is not a coordinate derivative.

For an ordered triangle, the formula is ∂[v0,v1,v2]=[v1,v2]−[v0,v2]+[v0,v1].\partial[v_0,v_1,v_2] =[v_1,v_2]-[v_0,v_2]+[v_0,v_1].∂[v0​,v1​,v2​]=[v1​,v2​]−[v0​,v2​]+[v0​,v1​]. The negative sign reverses the contribution of the middle edge. Over Z2\Z_2Z2​ the negative sign equals a positive sign. Repeated occurrences of an edge still have to be added in the chosen ring.

6.3 Cycles, boundaries and coefficients

A cycle is a chain with zero boundary. A boundary is a chain that is the boundary of a chain one dimension higher. Their groups and the resulting homology group are Zk(X;R)=ker⁡∂k,Bk(X;R)=im⁡∂k+1,Hk(X;R)=Zk(X;R)/Bk(X;R).Z_k(X;R)=\ker\partial_k,\qquad B_k(X;R)=\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1}, \qquad H_k(X;R)=Z_k(X;R)/B_k(X;R).Zk​(X;R)=ker∂k​,Bk​(X;R)=im∂k+1​,Hk​(X;R)=Zk​(X;R)/Bk​(X;R). The boundary-of-boundary theorem gives Bk⊆ZkB_k\subseteq Z_kBk​⊆Zk​, so the quotient is defined. Two cycles are homologous when their difference is a boundary. Homology records which cycles remain after this identification. The symbols ZkZ_kZk​ and BkB_kBk​ denote groups, distinct from the coefficient ring Z\ZZ and the braid group BnB_nBn​ of Lecture 5.

For example, take the boundary of a triangle, with no filled face. The sum of its cyclically directed edges is a cycle. A general cycle has equal coefficients on those three directed edges, since its boundary vanishes at each vertex. There are no two-dimensional chains, so this cycle generates H1≅RH_1\cong RH1​≅R. Filling the triangle makes that same sum a boundary and gives H1=0H_1=0H1​=0.

For a finite complex, order the simplices of each dimension. The boundary matrix DkD_kDk​ has a column for each source kkk-simplex and a row for each target (k−1)(k-1)(k−1)-simplex. Its entries are the signed face coefficients. Write ckc_kck​ for the number of kkk-simplices and rkr_krk​ for the rank of DkD_kDk​ over a specified field. Rank–nullity and the dimension formula for a quotient, recalled from Linear Algebra for Physics, give dim⁡Hk(X;R)=(ck−rk)−rk+1when R is a field.\dim H_k(X;R)=(c_k-r_k)-r_{k+1}\qquad\text{when $R$ is a field}.dimHk​(X;R)=(ck​−rk​)−rk+1​when R is a field. Over Z\ZZ, integer relations determine an abelian-group quotient instead. Real row reduction alone does not determine that group.

An element is a torsion element if it is nonzero and some positive integer multiple is zero. For an algebraic example, take the chain complex with C2=C1=ZC_2=C_1=\ZC2​=C1​=Z, boundary ∂2\partial_2∂2​ given by multiplication by two, and ∂1=0\partial_1=0∂1​=0. Its first homology is Z/2Z\Z/2\ZZ/2Z. With real coefficients, multiplication by two is onto, so the first homology is zero. With Z2\Z_2Z2​ coefficients it is the zero map, so the first homology is Z2\Z_2Z2​. This illustrates why coefficients are part of the answer. For a finite complex, the kkkth Betti number means bk=dim⁡RHk(X;R)b_k=\dim_{\R}H_k(X;\R)bk​=dimR​Hk​(X;R). Real coefficients discard torsion information.

For a single filled triangle, the counts are 333, 333 and 111, giving χ=1\Eulerchar=1χ=1. Its homology has one degree-zero generator and no higher generators, which gives the same alternating sum.

6.5 Invariance and closed surfaces

The calculation uses a decomposition. Its independence of that choice requires a theorem, rather than a comparison of a few examples.

Theorem 6.3 (Homology invariancecited). The homology of a Δ\DeltaΔ-complex agrees with its singular homology. Homotopy equivalences induce isomorphisms of these groups, with the coefficient ring fixed. Consequently the Euler characteristic of a finite Δ\DeltaΔ-complex is invariant under homotopy equivalence.

The comparison is Hatcher, Theorem 2.27. Homotopy invariance is Corollary 2.11; coefficients are treated in §2.2, pp. 153–155. Singular homology uses all continuous maps Δk→X\Delta^k\to XΔk→X as generators, without the interior-injectivity requirement. That construction applies without choosing a decomposition. The Euler assertion follows from the theorem above, for example with real coefficients.

A triangulation is a presentation of a surface by triangles glued along edges. A triangulated surface is orientable if its triangles can be oriented so that their induced directions on every shared edge are opposite. We use as part of the cited surface classification below that every closed surface admits such a presentation and that this condition is independent of the choice. The torus has this property; the projective plane does not.

Theorem 6.4 (Closed surfacescited). Closed connected surfaces are classified, up to homeomorphism, by their orientability and Euler characteristic. For the orientable surface Σg\genussurface gΣg​ of genus g≥0g\ge0g≥0, the Euler characteristic is 2−2g2-2g2−2g, where Σ0=S2\genussurface0=\sphere2Σ0​=S2.

This is the classification theorem of Kosniowski, Theorem 11.3, together with the Euler counts in Hatcher, §2.2, pp. 146–147. For g≥1g\ge1g≥1, the polygon model of Lecture 5 has one vertex, 2g2g2g edges and one face. Using such polygonal cells instead of triangles gives the same Euler characteristic by Hatcher, Theorem 2.44. Thus its count is 1−2g+11-2g+11−2g+1.

There is also a direct connection to Lecture 5. For any path-connected space, H1(X;Z)H_1(X;\Z)H1​(X;Z) is the abelianization of π1(X)\fundamentalgroup(X)π1​(X): the quotient by the normal subgroup generated by all commutators aba−1b−1aba^{-1}b^{-1}aba−1b−1. This result is cited from Hatcher, Theorem 2A.1. Homology therefore forgets the order information in noncommuting loop classes. It need not recover the fundamental group.

6.7 Binary cycles and the toric code

On a square lattice with opposite sides identified, put one qubit on each edge. A qubit has basis states ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩\ket1∣1⟩. Its phase-flip operator ZZZ fixes ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ and multiplies ∣1⟩\ket1∣1⟩ by −1-1−1. This operator symbol is distinct from the cycle group ZkZ_kZk​. A ZZZ-string applies this operator on a chosen set of edges. Represent that set by a one-chain with Z2\Z_2Z2​ coefficients: an edge has coefficient one exactly when it is selected. Adding chains means toggling the edges, since applying ZZZ twice is the identity.

The relevant local operations are read from vertices and square faces, called plaquettes. A vertex condition on a string requires an even number of selected edges at that vertex. Algebraically this is ∂c=0\partial c=0∂c=0. A plaquette boundary is also a cycle. Adding it replaces a part of the string by the other edges of that face. The drawing shows this change beside two independent closed strings.

Draw every square’s diagonal from lower left to upper right and order its triangles as in the torus example. A triangle boundary replaces each added diagonal in a cycle by its other two edges. Conversely, each diagonal occurs in exactly the two triangles of its square. A binary sum of triangle boundaries with no diagonal term therefore uses the two triangles of each square with equal coefficients. The two triangle boundaries add to the square boundary, so the sum is a sum of square boundaries. This proves that square-lattice cycles modulo square boundaries agree with the homology of this triangulation. By the cited Theorem 6.3, its homology agrees with that of the two-triangle torus in Example 6.5.

Example 6.7 (Toric-code string classescited; two-triangle homology proved). The binary string classes form H1(T2;Z2)≅Z22H_1(T^2;\Z_2)\cong\Z_2^2H1​(T2;Z2​)≅Z22​. Representatives wind once in the horizontal and vertical directions. Every class is a sum of these two, so there are four classes. The identification of these lattice representatives is part of Kitaev’s cited construction.

The code subspace is a chosen subspace of the joint qubit state space used to store quantum information. A logical operator preserves that subspace and acts on the states stored there. In the toric code, closed ZZZ-strings preserve the code subspace. Plaquette boundaries act as the identity on that subspace, so homologous strings act in the same way. The two independent classes give the two logical ZZZ operators. The full code construction supplies corresponding complementary operators and a four-dimensional code subspace: two logical qubits. This physical interpretation is cited from Kitaev (2003), §1, equations (1)–(2) and the following logical-operator argument. The homology calculation identifies the string classes; the Hilbert-space and operator assertions use that cited construction.

Self-check.

Compute the Euler characteristic of the sphere from the boundary of a tetrahedron and from the boundary of an octahedron.

Answer.

The tetrahedron has four vertices. Every pair is an edge, giving (42)=6\binom42=6(24​)=6 edges, and every triple is a face, giving four triangles. The count is 4−6+4=24-6+4=24−6+4=2. The octahedron has vertices at the positive and negative coordinate-axis unit vectors. Every vertex joins the four vertices other than itself and its opposite, giving 6⋅4/2=126\cdot4/2=126⋅4/2=12 edges. A face chooses one endpoint from each axis, giving 23=82^3=823=8 triangles. Its count is 6−12+8=26-12+8=26−12+8=2, in agreement with the tetrahedron.

Summary

The boundary map has square zero. Homology is the quotient of cycles by boundaries, with the coefficient ring specified. Over a field, boundary-matrix ranks give its dimensions and their alternating sum is the Euler characteristic. Invariance and surface classification are cited. The torus has two independent binary one-cycle classes, which describe the two logical ZZZ-string classes in the toric code. Lecture 7 adds smooth coordinates to spaces so that calculus can be defined on them.
