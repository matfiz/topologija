Lecture 6

Homology and the Euler characteristic

What this lecture does

Homology distinguishes the sphere from a point using the invariance theorem of §6.5. Both spaces have trivial fundamental group (Lecture 4). Homology compares closed chains with boundaries to count holes in each dimension. We compute it from boundary matrices for the torus and projective plane. Its alternating dimensions give the Euler characteristic. Binary cycle classes also describe closed strings in the toric code.

6.1 Simplices and their identifications

Definition 6.1 (Simplex). A kkk-simplex is the convex hull of k+1k+1k+1 affinely independent points, with a chosen ordering of these vertices. The standard simplex is Δk={(t0,…,tk)∈Rk+1:ti≥0, t0+⋯+tk=1}.\Delta^k=\{(t_0,\ldots,t_k)\in\R^{k+1}:t_i\ge0,\ t_0+\cdots+t_k=1\}.Δk={(t0​,…,tk​)∈Rk+1:ti​≥0, t0​+⋯+tk​=1}. Deleting one vertex gives a face, with its inherited order.

A point, an interval and a triangle are simplices of dimensions zero, one and two. The order of the vertices orients an edge from the first vertex to the second. It also specifies the signs of higher-dimensional faces.

We identify the endpoints of one interval, as in Lecture 2, to obtain a circle. The domain endpoints v0v_0v0​ and v1v_1v1​ are distinct, but both map to the same vertex vvv. The interval interior maps injectively to the open edge. This circle has one vertex and one edge. The quotient map q ⁣:Δ1→S1q\colon\Delta^1\to\sphere1q:Δ1→S1 sends the interval to the circle in the drawing, which also shows a three-edge decomposition.

Definition 6.2 (Δ\DeltaΔ-complex). A Δ\DeltaΔ-complex structure on XXX is a collection of continuous maps σ ⁣:Δk→X\sigma\colon\Delta^k\to Xσ:Δk→X, called its simplices, with the following properties. Each map is injective on the interior of its domain. These interiors have disjoint images and together cover XXX. The restriction to each face is a designated lower-dimensional simplex, using the affine identification preserving the vertex order. The topology is specified by these maps: a subset A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X is closed exactly when σ−1(A)\sigma^{-1}(A)σ−1(A) is closed for every simplex σ\sigmaσ.

The closed-set condition gives the quotient topology of Lecture 2. Thus the space is obtained by attaching the faces in the specified way.

Different vertices of one domain simplex may have the same image in XXX. An edge can therefore become a loop. By contrast, in a simplicial complex each simplex has distinct vertices in the space and any two simplices intersect in a common face or not at all. The torus model below has one vertex and two triangles. It is a Δ\DeltaΔ-complex, not a simplicial complex. Vertex names such as [v0,v1,v2][v_0,v_1,v_2][v0​,v1​,v2​] refer to ordered domain vertices even when some images coincide.

6.2 Chains and the boundary map

Fix a coefficient ring RRR. Our default is R=ZR=\ZR=Z. The alternatives used here are Z2\Z_2Z2​, the field with arithmetic modulo two, and the real field R\RR. Integers form a ring, not a field.

Definition 6.3 (Chains). A kkk-chain is a finite formal sum of the kkk-simplices with coefficients in RRR. These sums form the chain group Ck(X;R)C_k(X;R)Ck​(X;R).

6.3 Cycles, boundaries and coefficients

Definition 6.7 (Cycles). A cycle is a chain with zero boundary. Its group is Zk(X;R)=ker⁡∂k.Z_k(X;R)=\ker\partial_k.Zk​(X;R)=ker∂k​.

Definition 6.8 (Boundaries). A boundary is a chain that is the boundary of a chain one dimension higher. Its group is Bk(X;R)=im⁡∂k+1.B_k(X;R)=\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1}.Bk​(X;R)=im∂k+1​.

The boundary-of-boundary theorem gives Bk⊆ZkB_k\subseteq Z_kBk​⊆Zk​.

Definition 6.9 (Homology). The homology group in degree kkk is Hk(X;R)=Zk(X;R)/Bk(X;R).H_k(X;R)=Z_k(X;R)/B_k(X;R).Hk​(X;R)=Zk​(X;R)/Bk​(X;R). Two cycles are homologous when their difference is a boundary.

Homology records which cycles remain after this identification. The symbols ZkZ_kZk​ and BkB_kBk​ denote groups, distinct from the coefficient ring Z\ZZ and the braid group BnB_nBn​ of Lecture 5.

For example, take the boundary of a triangle, with no filled face. The sum zzz of its cyclically directed edges is a cycle. A general cycle has equal coefficients on those three directed edges, since its boundary vanishes at each vertex. There are no two-dimensional chains, so this cycle generates H1≅RH_1\cong RH1​≅R. Filling the triangle makes that same sum a boundary and gives H1=0H_1=0H1​=0.

Since ∂0=0\partial_0=0∂0​=0, every zero-chain is a cycle. The boundary of an edge from viv_ivi​ to vjv_jvj​ is vj−viv_j-v_ivj​−vi​, so the endpoint classes become equal in H0H_0H0​. Edge paths therefore identify all vertices in one component.

In a finite Δ\DeltaΔ-complex, these edge-path components are exactly the path components: each simplex joins its vertices by edges, and its points by paths to those vertices. Distinct edge-path components are disjoint finite unions of closed simplices, so they are also open.

Summing vertex coefficients separately in each component shows that there is no relation between different components. Thus H0H_0H0​ has one independent generator for each path component. Over Z\ZZ this is a free abelian group. Over a field it is a vector space.

The one-edge circle has zero edge boundary and no two-simplices, so its zeroth and first homology groups are both RRR. The triangle outline has the same answer. Filling changes its first homology but not its component count, as the following panels show.

6.4 The Euler characteristic

The alternating simplex count can be computed without reducing boundary matrices. We relate it to homology to explain why a different decomposition gives the same count.

6.5 Invariance and closed surfaces

The calculation uses a decomposition. Its independence of that choice requires a theorem, rather than a comparison of a few examples.

Theorem 6.14 (Homology invariancecited). The homology of a Δ\DeltaΔ-complex agrees with its singular homology. Homotopy equivalences induce isomorphisms of these groups, with the coefficient ring fixed. Consequently the Euler characteristic of a finite Δ\DeltaΔ-complex is invariant under homotopy equivalence.

The comparison is Hatcher, Theorem 2.27. Homotopy invariance is Corollary 2.11. Coefficients are treated in §2.2, pp. 153–155. Singular homology uses all continuous maps Δk→X\Delta^k\to XΔk→X as generators, without the interior-injectivity requirement. That construction applies without choosing a decomposition. The Euler assertion follows from the theorem above, for example with real coefficients.

Definition 6.15 (Triangulation). A triangulation is a presentation of a surface by triangles glued along edges.

Definition 6.16 (Orientability). A triangulated surface is orientable if its triangles can be oriented so that their induced directions on every shared edge are opposite.

We use as part of the cited surface classification below that every closed surface admits such a presentation and that this condition is independent of the choice. The torus has this property. The projective plane fails the sign test in §6.6.

The Möbius band of Lecture 2 illustrates the obstruction. After rescaling the transverse coordinate to t∈[−1,1]t\in[-1,1]t∈[−1,1], its rectangle model identifies (1,t)(1,t)(1,t) with (0,−t)(0,-t)(0,−t). Orienting adjacent triangles consistently in the rectangle makes the induced directions opposite on their interior edges. At the seam, the reversal of ttt makes the induced directions agree instead. Reversing every triangle changes both seam directions and cannot fix this. The drawing compares an interior edge with this seam.

Theorem 6.17 (Closed surfacescited). Closed connected surfaces are classified, up to homeomorphism, by their orientability and Euler characteristic. For the orientable surface Σg\genussurface gΣg​ of genus g≥0g\ge0g≥0, the Euler characteristic is 2−2g2-2g2−2g, where Σ0=S2\genussurface0=\sphere2Σ0​=S2.

This is the classification theorem of Kosniowski, Theorem 11.3, together with the Euler counts in Hatcher, §2.2, pp. 146–147. For g≥1g\ge1g≥1, the polygon model of Lecture 5 has one vertex, 2g2g2g edges and one face. Using such polygonal cells instead of triangles gives the same Euler characteristic by Hatcher, Theorem 2.44. Thus its count is 1−2g+11-2g+11−2g+1.

There is also a direct connection to Lecture 5. For any path-connected space, H1(X;Z)H_1(X;\Z)H1​(X;Z) is the abelianization of π1(X)\fundamentalgroup(X)π1​(X): the quotient by the normal subgroup generated by all commutators aba−1b−1aba^{-1}b^{-1}aba−1b−1. This result is cited from Hatcher, Theorem 2A.1. Homology therefore forgets the order information in noncommuting loop classes. It need not recover the fundamental group.

Thus the Klein bottle and torus have the same Euler characteristic. Their different orientability explains why the classification needs both data.

6.7 Binary cycles and the toric code

We use homology to distinguish strings that leave stored information unchanged from strings that can change it. The model is Kitaev’s toric code (arXiv:quant-ph/9707021v1, §1, equations (1)–(2)).

Physical input.

In the ideal finite-qubit model, normalized vectors in a complex tensor product describe pure states. A qubit has orthonormal basis ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩\ket1∣1⟩. Measuring a Hermitian operator on an eigenstate returns its eigenvalue. We interpret the joint +1+1+1 eigenspace defined below as the states used to store quantum information, following Kitaev, §1.

The phase-flip operator ZZZ and bit-flip operator XXX are given by Z∣0⟩=∣0⟩,Z∣1⟩=−∣1⟩,X∣0⟩=∣1⟩,X∣1⟩=∣0⟩.Z\ket0=\ket0,\qquad Z\ket1=-\ket1,\qquad X\ket0=\ket1,\qquad X\ket1=\ket0.Z∣0⟩=∣0⟩,Z∣1⟩=−∣1⟩,X∣0⟩=∣1⟩,X∣1⟩=∣0⟩. Thus X2=Z2=1X^2=Z^2=1X2=Z2=1 and XZ=−ZXXZ=-ZXXZ=−ZX, as follows by applying them to both basis vectors. The operator ZZZ is distinct from the cycle group ZkZ_kZk​.

Definition 6.20 (Toric code). Take an N×NN\times NN×N square lattice with opposite sides identified, where N≥3N\ge3N≥3. Put one qubit on each edge. The joint state space is H=(C2)⊗2N2.\mathcal H=(\C^2)^{\otimes 2N^2}.H=(C2)⊗2N2. The notation XeX_eXe​ and ZeZ_eZe​ means that the operator acts on edge eee and as the identity on every other factor. Adding binary chains toggles selected edges, since Ze2=1Z_e^2=1Ze2​=1. At a vertex vvv, commuting AvA_vAv​ past the string gives AvZ(c)=(−1)(∂c)vZ(c)Av.A_vZ(c)=(-1)^{(\partial c)_v}Z(c)A_v.Av​Z(c)=(−1)(∂c)v​Z(c)Av​. The binary record of violated conditions is the syndrome of a ZZZ-type error. It is precisely ∂c\partial c∂c. Along a simple open string only the two ends violate star conditions. Every ZZZ-string commutes with every BpB_pBp​. Consequently strings with ∂c=0\partial c=0∂c=0 preserve C\mathcal CC.

A face-boundary string is Z(∂p)=BpZ(\partial p)=B_pZ(∂p)=Bp​, which acts as the identity on C\mathcal CC. Products of these operators show that any boundary acts as the identity. Homologous closed strings therefore act alike on the code. The drawing separates the local stabilizers from open and closed strings.

The periodic cycles and a plaquette change can also be drawn on the full fundamental square.

The lattice uses square faces, whereas our homology was defined using simplices. We compare the two quotients by adding diagonals. We draw every square’s diagonal from lower left to upper right and order its triangles as in the torus example. A triangle boundary replaces each added diagonal in a cycle by its other two edges. Conversely, each diagonal occurs in exactly the two triangles of its square. A binary sum of triangle boundaries with no diagonal term therefore uses the two triangles of each square with equal coefficients. The two triangle boundaries add to the square boundary, so the sum is a sum of square boundaries. This proves that square-lattice cycles modulo square boundaries agree with the homology of this triangulation. By the cited Theorem 6.14, its homology agrees with that of the two-triangle torus in Example 6.18.

Example 6.23 (Toric-code string classescited; two-triangle homology proved). The binary string classes form H1(T2;Z2)≅Z22H_1(T^2;\Z_2)\cong\Z_2^2H1​(T2;Z2​)≅Z22​. Representatives wind once in the horizontal and vertical directions. Every class is a sum of a subset of these two generator classes, including the empty sum. There are therefore four classes. The square-to-triangle comparison was proved above. Its comparison with the two-triangle torus uses the cited homology-invariance theorem. The identification of these lattice representatives with logical ZZZ operators is Kitaev’s construction, with the parity calculation given here.

We can also derive the dimension of C\mathcal CC. There are N2N^2N2 stars and N2N^2N2 plaquettes, with relations ∏vAv=1,∏pBp=1.\prod_v A_v=1,\qquad \prod_p B_p=1.v∏​Av​=1,p∏​Bp​=1. Each edge occurs twice in each product. These are the only relations within each family. A product of selected stars is the identity only if the selection agrees at both ends of every edge. The vertex graph is connected, so either every vertex or no vertex is selected.

The same argument on the connected dual graph, whose vertices are faces and whose edges join neighbouring faces, applies to plaquettes. A product of XXX operators and a product of ZZZ operators can multiply to a scalar identity only when both supports are empty. Hence there are r=2N2−2r=2N^2-2r=2N2−2 independent conditions, with no relation giving −1-1−1.

For independent commuting stabilizers S1,…,SrS_1,\ldots,S_rS1​,…,Sr​, the projector onto their joint +1+1+1 eigenspace is P=2−r∏j=1r(1+Sj).P=2^{-r}\prod_{j=1}^r(1+S_j).P=2−rj=1∏r​(1+Sj​). Each factor (1+Sj)/2(1+S_j)/2(1+Sj​)/2 is the projector onto its +1+1+1 eigenspace. Every nonidentity product in the expansion has, on some edge, a factor XXX, ZZZ or XZXZXZ, up to a sign. Each of these has trace zero. The trace of a tensor product is the product of its factor traces. Only the identity contributes to the trace. Thus dim⁡C=tr⁡P=22N2−r=4.\dim\mathcal C=\operatorname{tr}P=2^{2N^2-r}=4.dimC=trP=22N2−r=4. This is the state-space dimension of two logical qubits. The complementary logical operators come from XXX-strings on the dual lattice, as in Kitaev, §1. A closed dual string applies XXX on the primal edges it crosses. It meets each plaquette boundary evenly, so it preserves C\mathcal CC. For each of the horizontal and vertical wrapping ZZZ-strings, we choose a closed dual string crossing it once. The two operators anticommute, so the wrapping ZZZ-string cannot act as the identity on C\mathcal CC.

The distinction matters for errors in this ideal model. A ZZZ-type error supported on fewer than NNN edges either has a nonzero syndrome or is a boundary and acts identically on the code. For the length bound, decompose a binary cycle into closed edge walks. A walk with nonzero winding lifts to a lattice walk whose endpoint displacement has a coordinate of magnitude at least NNN. It therefore needs at least NNN edges. A cycle of smaller total length has no wrapping component and is a boundary. This is the ZZZ-string part of Kitaev’s error-detection argument in §1. The interpretation as a quantum code and the complementary operators remain those of the cited construction.

Summary

The boundary map has square zero. Homology is the quotient of cycles by boundaries, with the coefficient ring specified. Over a field, boundary-matrix ranks give its dimensions and their alternating sum is the Euler characteristic. Invariance and surface classification are cited. The torus has two independent binary one-cycle classes, which describe the two logical ZZZ-string classes in the toric code. Lecture 7 adds smooth coordinates to spaces so that calculus can be defined on them.
