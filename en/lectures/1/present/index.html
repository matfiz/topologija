Lecture 1

Spaces and continuity

Continuity can be described without measuring distances. We keep the open sets of a metric space and use them to define a topological space. We then prove that metric continuity is equivalent to openness of inverse images. This gives a way to compare spaces and to put a topology on a subset or a product. Explicit maps identify an interval with a line and an open disc with a plane.

1.2 Topological spaces and bases

Definition 1.2 (Topology). A topology on a set XXX is a collection T\topoT of subsets of XXX that contains ∅\varnothing∅ and XXX and is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. The pair (X,T)(X,\topo)(X,T) is a topological space. Its members are called open sets. A subset is closed if its complement in XXX is open.

The preceding proposition constructs the metric topology of ddd. The standard topology on Rn\R^nRn is the topology of the Euclidean distance. We use this topology unless another one is specified. The same set may carry other topologies. The discrete topology contains every subset. The indiscrete topology contains only ∅\varnothing∅ and XXX. Both satisfy the axioms directly.

A set can be both open and closed. The sets XXX and ∅\varnothing∅ always are. A set can be neither open nor closed. The set [0,1)[0,1)[0,1) in the standard real line is one example. Its complement is not open at 111.

For a subset A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X, its interior int⁡A\interior AintA is the union of all open subsets of AAA. Its closure A‾\closure AA is the intersection of all closed sets containing AAA. The interior is open by the union axiom. The closure is closed because its complement is a union of open sets. Every open subset of AAA is one of the sets being united, so lies in int⁡A\interior AintA. Every closed superset of AAA contains the defining intersection, so contains A‾\closure AA.

A neighbourhood of x∈Xx\in Xx∈X is a set containing an open set that contains xxx. A neighbourhood itself need not be open.

In the standard real line, int⁡[0,1)=(0,1)\interior[0,1)=(0,1)int[0,1)=(0,1) and [0,1)‾=[0,1]\closure{[0,1)}=[0,1][0,1)​=[0,1]. Listing all open sets is usually unnecessary. Open balls already determine the metric topology because every open set is a union of balls.

In Rn\R^nRn, boxes with rational endpoints give a countable basis as well. Every open ball contains such a box about each of its points, and every open box contains a ball about each of its points. Thus boxes and balls give the same topology. Second countability will be a condition in the definition of a manifold in Lecture 7.

1.4 Subspaces and products

The circle S1={(x,y)∈R2:x2+y2=1}\sphere1=\{(x,y)\in\R^2:x^2+y^2=1\}S1={(x,y)∈R2:x2+y2=1} is a subset of the plane. Its open sets must describe motion along the circle, although no plane ball lies inside the circle.

Definition 1.8 (Subspace and product topologies). The subspace topology on A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X consists of the sets A∩UA\cap UA∩U with UUU open in XXX. For Rn\R^nRn, every product of open sets is a union of open boxes, by choosing an interval about each coordinate. The box-and-ball comparison therefore identifies the finite product and Euclidean topologies.

1.5 Hausdorff spaces and invariants

Definition 1.10 (Hausdorff space). A space XXX is Hausdorff if any two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods. A sequence (xn)n≥1(x_n)_{n\ge1}(xn​)n≥1​ converges to x∈Xx\in Xx∈X if every open neighbourhood of xxx contains all xnx_nxn​ for sufficiently large nnn.

Proposition 1.11 (Separation and limits). Metric spaces are Hausdorff. Subspaces and finite products of Hausdorff spaces are Hausdorff. A sequence in a Hausdorff space has at most one limit.

Proof. For distinct points xxx and yyy of a metric space, put r=d(x,y)/3r=d(x,y)/3r=d(x,y)/3. The balls Br(x)B_r(x)Br​(x) and Br(y)B_r(y)Br​(y) are disjoint. A point in both would give d(x,y)<2r<d(x,y)d(x,y)<2r<d(x,y)d(x,y)<2r<d(x,y) by the triangle inequality. Intersecting disjoint open neighbourhoods with a subspace separates its points. Two distinct points in a product differ in some coordinate. Separate those coordinate values and take inverse images under the corresponding projection. The resulting product neighbourhoods are disjoint.

If a sequence had distinct limits xxx and yyy, disjoint open neighbourhoods UUU and VVV of those points would both contain every term after some common index. This would put a term in U∩V=∅U\cap V=\varnothingU∩V=∅. 

The Hausdorff condition can fail. The cofinite topology on R\RR consists of ∅\varnothing∅ and the subsets with finite complement. It is a topology. A nonempty union has complement contained in the finite complement of any one nonempty member. A finite intersection of nonempty open sets has complement a finite union of finite sets. Two nonempty open sets cannot be disjoint, since their intersection has finite complement in the infinite set R\RR. This space is not Hausdorff.

A topological invariant is a property shared by homeomorphic spaces. Hausdorffness is one example.

Proposition 1.12 (Transport of open sets). A homeomorphism f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y gives a bijection U↦f(U)U\mapsto f(U)U↦f(U) between their topologies, preserving unions, intersections and inclusion. Hausdorffness and second countability are topological invariants.

Proof. If f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y is a homeomorphism and U⊆XU\subseteq XU⊆X is open, then f(U)=(f−1)−1(U)f(U)=(f^{-1})^{-1}(U)f(U)=(f−1)−1(U) is open by continuity of the inverse. Continuity of fff gives the converse correspondence. A bijection preserves the stated set operations. Images of disjoint open neighbourhoods separate the image points, proving Hausdorff invariance. If B\basisB is a countable basis for XXX, its images form a countable family of open sets. Bijectivity carries the covering and refinement properties across. Every open set of YYY is the image of a union of members of B\basisB, hence a union of these image sets. They form a basis for YYY. 

The standard real line and the cofinite real line are therefore not homeomorphic. They have the same underlying set but different separation properties.

Summary

A topology records which subsets are open. Continuity means that inverse images of open sets are open, and agrees with metric continuity when a metric is present. A homeomorphism requires continuity in both directions. Subspace and product topologies let us test continuity using inclusion and coordinate projections. Hausdorffness is preserved by homeomorphisms and ensures uniqueness of sequence limits. Lecture 2 introduces compactness and connectedness to distinguish more spaces.

References

Munkres, Topology, second edition, §§12–13 and 15–18: topologies, bases, products, subspaces, separation and continuity.
