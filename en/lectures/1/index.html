Lecture 1

Spaces and continuity

What this lecture does

We compare spaces using bijections that are continuous in both directions. An open interval and the whole line are the same in this sense, despite their different lengths. A line and a plane are different, as Lecture 2 proves. We define continuity using open sets, without choosing a distance. We then construct such bijections for an interval and a line, a punctured circle and a line, and an open disc and a plane.

1.2 Topological spaces and bases

Definition 1.5 (Topology). A topology on a set XXX is a collection T\topoT of subsets of XXX that contains ∅\varnothing∅ and XXX and is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. The pair (X,T)(X,\topo)(X,T) is a topological space. Its members are called open sets.

Definition 1.6 (Closed set). A subset of a topological space XXX is closed if its complement in XXX is open.

The preceding proposition constructs the metric topology of ddd. The standard topology on Rn\R^nRn is the topology of the Euclidean distance. We use this topology unless another one is specified. The same set may carry other topologies.

Definition 1.7 (Discrete topology). The discrete topology on a set XXX contains every subset of XXX.

Definition 1.8 (Indiscrete topology). The indiscrete topology on a set XXX contains only ∅\varnothing∅ and XXX.

Both satisfy the axioms directly.

A set can be both open and closed. The sets XXX and ∅\varnothing∅ always are. A set can be neither open nor closed. The set [0,1)[0,1)[0,1) in the standard real line is one example. Its complement is not open at 111.

Definition 1.9 (Neighbourhood). A neighbourhood of x∈Xx\in Xx∈X is a set containing an open set that contains xxx.

A neighbourhood itself need not be open.

Definition 1.10 (Interior). For a subset A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X, its interior int⁡A\interior AintA is the union of all open subsets of AAA.

Definition 1.11 (Closure). For a subset A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X, its closure A‾\closure AA is the intersection of all closed sets containing AAA.

The interior is open by the union axiom. The closure is closed because its complement is a union of open sets. Every open subset of AAA is one of the sets being united, so lies in int⁡A\interior AintA. Every closed superset of AAA contains the defining intersection, so contains A‾\closure AA. Thus the interior is the largest open subset of AAA, and the closure is the smallest closed superset of AAA.

The closure describes approximation using open sets.

Proposition 1.12 (Closure criterion). For A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X, a point x∈Xx\in Xx∈X lies in A‾\closure AA exactly when every open set containing xxx meets AAA.

Proof. If an open set UUU contains xxx and misses AAA, its complement is a closed superset of AAA omitting xxx. Hence x∉A‾x\notin\closure Ax∈/A. Conversely, if x∉A‾x\notin\closure Ax∈/A, some closed superset CCC of AAA omits xxx. Its complement is an open set containing xxx and missing AAA. 

A topology therefore determines which points can be approximated by each subset, without assigning distances. In the discrete topology, a point outside a set has the singleton containing that point as an open neighbourhood missing the set. Thus every subset of a discrete space is its own closure. In the indiscrete topology, every open neighbourhood of any point meets every nonempty set. Thus every nonempty subset of an indiscrete space has the whole space as its closure.

To check the second equality, note that every open interval about 111 meets [0,1)[0,1)[0,1), so no closed superset can omit 111. The closed set [0,1][0,1][0,1] itself contains [0,1)[0,1)[0,1). This is a size restriction: every open set is a union of members of one countable family.

Thus boxes and balls give the same topology. Second countability will be a condition in the definition of a manifold in Lecture 7.

1.4 Subspaces and products

The circle S1={(x,y)∈R2:x2+y2=1}\sphere1=\{(x,y)\in\R^2:x^2+y^2=1\}S1={(x,y)∈R2:x2+y2=1} is a subset of the plane. Its open sets must describe motion along the circle, although no plane ball lies inside the circle.

Definition 1.20 (Subspace topology). The subspace topology on A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X consists of the sets A∩UA\cap UA∩U with UUU open in XXX.

Intersections with AAA preserve unions and finite intersections, so the subspace sets form a topology. Rectangles cover the product, and the intersection of two rectangles is a rectangle. Hence the basis proposition constructs the product topology.

The restricted metric has balls A∩Br(x)A\cap B_r(x)A∩Br​(x) for x∈Ax\in Ax∈A. They are subspace-open. Conversely, if x∈A∩Ux\in A\cap Ux∈A∩U with UUU open in XXX, a ball Br(x)⊆UB_r(x)\subseteq UBr​(x)⊆U gives a restricted ball inside A∩UA\cap UA∩U. Thus the two topologies agree.

For Rn\R^nRn, every product of open sets is a union of open boxes, by choosing an interval about each coordinate. The box-and-ball comparison therefore identifies the finite product and Euclidean topologies.

1.5 Hausdorff spaces and invariants

Limits in a metric space are unique. We identify the separation property of open sets that ensures this uniqueness without a metric.

Definition 1.23 (Hausdorff space). A space XXX is Hausdorff if any two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods.

Definition 1.24 (Convergence). A sequence (xn)n≥1(x_n)_{n\ge1}(xn​)n≥1​ converges to x∈Xx\in Xx∈X if every open neighbourhood of xxx contains all xnx_nxn​ for sufficiently large nnn.

Proposition 1.25 (Separation and limits). Metric spaces are Hausdorff. Subspaces and finite products of Hausdorff spaces are Hausdorff. A sequence in a Hausdorff space has at most one limit.

Proof. For distinct points xxx and yyy of a metric space, put r=d(x,y)/3r=d(x,y)/3r=d(x,y)/3. The balls Br(x)B_r(x)Br​(x) and Br(y)B_r(y)Br​(y) are disjoint. A point in both would give d(x,y)<2r<d(x,y)d(x,y)<2r<d(x,y)d(x,y)<2r<d(x,y) by the triangle inequality. Intersecting disjoint open neighbourhoods with a subspace separates its points. Two distinct points in a product differ in some coordinate. Separate those coordinate values and take inverse images under the corresponding projection. The resulting product neighbourhoods are disjoint.

If a sequence had distinct limits xxx and yyy, disjoint open neighbourhoods UUU and VVV of those points would both contain every term after some common index. This would put a term in U∩V=∅U\cap V=\varnothingU∩V=∅. 

The Hausdorff condition can fail.

Definition 1.26 (Cofinite topology). The cofinite topology on R\RR consists of ∅\varnothing∅ and the subsets with finite complement.

It is a topology. A nonempty union has complement contained in the finite complement of any one nonempty member. A finite intersection of nonempty open sets has complement a finite union of finite sets. Two nonempty open sets cannot be disjoint, since their intersection has finite complement in the infinite set R\RR. This space is not Hausdorff. The sequence given by xn=nx_n=nxn​=n converges to every x∈Rx\in\Rx∈R in this topology. The terms of this sequence are distinct. Every open neighbourhood of xxx omits only finitely many real numbers, so it omits only finitely many terms of the sequence. Thus limits need not be unique without the Hausdorff property.

Definition 1.27 (Topological invariant). A topological invariant is a property shared by homeomorphic spaces.

Hausdorffness is one example.

Proposition 1.28 (Transport of open sets). A homeomorphism f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y gives a bijection U↦f(U)U\mapsto f(U)U↦f(U) between their topologies, preserving unions, intersections and inclusion. Hausdorffness and second countability are topological invariants.

Proof. If f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y is a homeomorphism and U⊆XU\subseteq XU⊆X is open, then f(U)=(f−1)−1(U)f(U)=(f^{-1})^{-1}(U)f(U)=(f−1)−1(U) is open by continuity of the inverse. Continuity of fff gives the converse correspondence. A bijection preserves the stated set operations. Images of disjoint open neighbourhoods separate the image points, proving Hausdorff invariance. If B\basisB is a countable basis for XXX, its images form a countable family of open sets. Bijectivity carries the covering and refinement properties across. Every open set of YYY is the image of a union of members of B\basisB, hence a union of these image sets. They form a basis for YYY. 

The standard real line and the cofinite real line are therefore not homeomorphic. They have the same underlying set but different separation properties.

Summary

A topology records which subsets are open. Continuity means that inverse images of open sets are open, and agrees with metric continuity when a metric is present. A homeomorphism requires continuity in both directions. Subspace and product topologies let us test continuity using inclusion and coordinate projections. Hausdorffness is preserved by homeomorphisms and ensures uniqueness of sequence limits. Lecture 2 introduces compactness and connectedness to distinguish more spaces.
