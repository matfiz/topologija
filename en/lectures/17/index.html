Lecture 17

Curvature

What this lecture does

Second covariant derivatives need not commute. Their difference, corrected for the bracket of the directions, defines curvature. We compute this tensor from Christoffel symbols and from differential forms in an orthonormal frame. The sphere fixes the sign used for surface geometry and gravitation.

17.1 The curvature tensor

Let MMM be a smooth manifold with an affine connection ∇\nabla∇. The curvature operator is defined on smooth fields by R(X,Y)Z=∇X∇YZ−∇Y∇XZ−∇[X,Y]Z.\Riemann(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z.R(X,Y)Z=∇X​∇Y​Z−∇Y​∇X​Z−∇[X,Y]​Z. For the Cartesian connection on Rn\R^nRn, its components are XY(Zk)−YX(Zk)−[X,Y](Zk)=0XY(Z^k)-YX(Z^k)-[X,Y](Z^k)=0XY(Zk)−YX(Zk)−[X,Y](Zk)=0. The bracket correction removes the failure of the direction fields to commute.

Theorem 17.1 (Tensoriality of curvature). Curvature is linear over C∞(M)C^\infty(M)C∞(M) in all three arguments. It therefore gives a smooth tensor of type (1,3)(1,3)(1,3), called the Riemann curvature tensor.

Proof. Real multilinearity is immediate from the connection rules. For f∈C∞(M)f\in C^\infty(M)f∈C∞(M), the Leibniz rule and [fX,Y]=f[X,Y]−Y(f)X[fX,Y]=f[X,Y]-Y(f)X[fX,Y]=f[X,Y]−Y(f)X give R(fX,Y)Z=fR(X,Y)Z−Y(f)∇XZ+Y(f)∇XZ=fR(X,Y)Z.\Riemann(fX,Y)Z=f\Riemann(X,Y)Z -Y(f)\nabla_XZ+Y(f)\nabla_XZ=f\Riemann(X,Y)Z.R(fX,Y)Z=fR(X,Y)Z−Y(f)∇X​Z+Y(f)∇X​Z=fR(X,Y)Z. Antisymmetry in XXX and YYY gives linearity in YYY. Expansion in the last slot gives R(X,Y)(fZ)=fR(X,Y)Z+(XY(f)−YX(f)−[X,Y](f))Z=fR(X,Y)Z.\Riemann(X,Y)(fZ)=f\Riemann(X,Y)Z +\bigl(XY(f)-YX(f)-[X,Y](f)\bigr)Z=f\Riemann(X,Y)Z.R(X,Y)(fZ)=fR(X,Y)Z+(XY(f)−YX(f)−[X,Y](f))Z=fR(X,Y)Z. The cross terms Y(f)∇XZY(f)\nabla_XZY(f)∇X​Z and X(f)∇YZX(f)\nabla_YZX(f)∇Y​Z cancel between the two second derivatives. By the cutoff and coordinate-expansion argument of §16.1, the value therefore depends only on the three vectors at the point. Smoothness follows from the local coefficients of the connection. 

Write ∂μ=∂/∂xμ\partial_\mu=\partial/\partial x^\mu∂μ​=∂/∂xμ in a chart. Greek indices here range from 111 to nnn. The index order is fixed by the action R(∂μ,∂ν)∂σ=Rρσμν∂ρ.\Riemann(\partial_\mu,\partial_\nu)\partial_\sigma =\Riemann^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\partial_\rho.R(∂μ​,∂ν​)∂σ​=Rρσμν​∂ρ​. Applying the connection product rule twice gives Rρσμν=∂μΓνσρ−∂νΓμσρ+ΓμλρΓνσλ−ΓνλρΓμσλ.\Riemann^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} =\partial_\mu\Christoffel^\rho_{\nu\sigma} -\partial_\nu\Christoffel^\rho_{\mu\sigma} +\Christoffel^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Christoffel^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} -\Christoffel^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Christoffel^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}.Rρσμν​=∂μ​Γνσρ​−∂ν​Γμσρ​+Γμλρ​Γνσλ​−Γνλρ​Γμσλ​. For example, applying the product rule gives ∇∂μ∇∂ν∂σ=(∂μΓνσρ+ΓνσλΓμλρ)∂ρ.\nabla_{\partial_\mu}\nabla_{\partial_\nu}\partial_\sigma =(\partial_\mu\Christoffel^\rho_{\nu\sigma} +\Christoffel^\lambda_{\nu\sigma}\Christoffel^\rho_{\mu\lambda})\partial_\rho.∇∂μ​​∇∂ν​​∂σ​=(∂μ​Γνσρ​+Γνσλ​Γμλρ​)∂ρ​. Subtract the reversed expression. The bracket term vanishes because coordinate fields commute. Curvature is a tensor even though the Christoffel symbols are not. For the Euclidean plane, Lecture 16 gives the polar symbols Γϕϕr=−r\Christoffel^r_{\phi\phi}=-rΓϕϕr​=−r and Γrϕϕ=Γϕrϕ=1/r\Christoffel^\phi_{r\phi}=\Christoffel^\phi_{\phi r}=1/rΓrϕϕ​=Γϕrϕ​=1/r. 17.2 Metric symmetries and the first Bianchi identity

Now let ∇\nabla∇ be the Levi-Civita connection of a pseudo-Riemannian metric. Proposition 17.2 (Curvature symmetriessketched; antisymmetries and first Bianchi proved). The curvature satisfies C(X,Y,Z,W)=−C(Y,X,Z,W)=−C(X,Y,W,Z),R(X,Y)Z+R(Y,Z)X+R(Z,X)Y=0,C(X,Y,Z,W)=C(Z,W,X,Y).\begin{split} C(X,Y,Z,W)&=-C(Y,X,Z,W)=-C(X,Y,W,Z),\\ \Riemann(X,Y)Z+\Riemann(Y,Z)X+\Riemann(Z,X)Y&=0,\\ C(X,Y,Z,W)&=C(Z,W,X,Y). \end{split}C(X,Y,Z,W)R(X,Y)Z+R(Y,Z)X+R(Z,X)YC(X,Y,Z,W)​=−C(Y,X,Z,W)=−C(X,Y,W,Z),=0,=C(Z,W,X,Y).​ The middle equation is the first Bianchi identity.

Proof and sketch. The first antisymmetry follows from the operator definition. For the other one, apply XY−YX−[X,Y]XY-YX-[X,Y]XY−YX−[X,Y] to g(Z,W)\metric(Z,W)g(Z,W). This proves antisymmetry in the last pair.

For Bianchi, sum cyclically over (X,Y,Z)(X,Y,Z)(X,Y,Z). Zero torsion first combines the second derivatives into ∑cyc∇X[Y,Z]\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\nabla_X[Y,Z]∑cyc​∇X​[Y,Z]. The last equality is Jacobi’s identity.

For pair exchange, write Bianchi for four cyclic permutations of the four fields. Add the identities and use the two antisymmetries to cancel terms. The remaining equality gives pair exchange after renaming the fields. This sketches the algebraic step; see Lee, Proposition 7.4, pp. 121–123. 

In the lowered-output convention, these identities give Rabcd=−Rbacd=−Rabdc\Riemann_{ab cd}=-\Riemann_{ba cd}=-\Riemann_{ab dc}Rabcd​=−Rbacd​=−Rabdc​ and Rabcd=Rcdab\Riemann_{ab cd}=\Riemann_{cd ab}Rabcd​=Rcdab​. The slot order must also be translated when comparing sources.1 Changing the operator sign would negate every curvature value below; changing slot order alone can also introduce a sign.

17.3 Ricci, scalar and sectional curvature

The Ricci tensor is the trace Ric⁡(X,Y)=tr⁡(Z↦R(Z,Y)X)\Ricci(X,Y)=\operatorname{tr}(Z\mapsto\Riemann(Z,Y)X)Ric(X,Y)=tr(Z↦R(Z,Y)X). The scalar curvature is its metric trace. In coordinates, Ric⁡μν=Rρμρν,Rsc=gμνRic⁡μν.\Ricci_{\mu\nu}=\Riemann^\rho{}_{\mu\rho\nu},\qquad \scalarcurv=\metric^{\mu\nu}\Ricci_{\mu\nu}.Ricμν​=Rρμρν​,Rsc​=gμνRicμν​. Pair exchange makes Ricci symmetric: in Ric⁡ij=gabRaibj\Ricci_{ij}=\metric^{ab}\Riemann_{a i b j}Ricij​=gabRaibj​, exchange the curvature pairs and then rename aaa and bbb. The symbol Rsc\scalarcurvRsc​ distinguishes scalar curvature from a sphere radius.

For a Riemannian metric and a tangent 2-plane P⊂TpMP\subset T_pMP⊂Tp​M, its sectional curvature is K(P)=g(R(u,v)v,u)g(u,u)g(v,v)−g(u,v)2,P=span⁡{u,v}.\Gausscurv(P)=\frac{\metric(\Riemann(u,v)v,u)} {\metric(u,u)\metric(v,v)-\metric(u,v)^2}, \qquad P=\Span\{u,v\}.K(P)=g(u,u)g(v,v)−g(u,v)2g(R(u,v)v,u)​,P=span{u,v}. Here uuu and vvv are linearly independent. The denominator is positive. On changing the basis of PPP, antisymmetry in both pairs multiplies the numerator by the square of the basis-change determinant. The Gram determinant in the denominator has the same factor. Thus the quotient is independent of the basis. For orthonormal uuu and vvv, it is g(R(u,v)v,u)\metric(\Riemann(u,v)v,u)g(R(u,v)v,u). In indefinite signature the same quotient defines sectional curvature only for nondegenerate planes, whose Gram determinant is nonzero.

Summary

Curvature is the tensor R(X,Y)=[∇X,∇Y]−∇[X,Y]\Riemann(X,Y)=[\nabla_X,\nabla_Y]-\nabla_{[X,Y]}R(X,Y)=[∇X​,∇Y​]−∇[X,Y]​. Metric compatibility and zero torsion give its antisymmetries and first Bianchi identity. Ricci and scalar curvature are its specified contractions. In a local orthonormal frame, Cartan’s equations replace Christoffel computations by dθ=−ω∧θ\dd\coframe=-\form\wedge\coframedθ=−ω∧θ and Ω=dω+ω∧ω\curvaturematrix=\dd\form+\form\wedge\formΩ=dω+ω∧ω. On a surface, dω12=Kθ1∧θ2\dd\surfaceconn=\Gausscurv\coframe^1\wedge\coframe^2dω12​=Kθ1∧θ2. The sphere gives positive curvature, the half-plane negative curvature, and the flat quotient torus and cylinder zero curvature.
