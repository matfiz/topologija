Curvature

What this lecture does

Curvature measures the change in a vector transported around a small loop. We compute it from Christoffel symbols and from differential forms in an orthonormal frame. The sphere fixes the sign used for surface geometry and gravitation.

17.1 The curvature tensor

Parallel transport compares tangent vectors along a path, as in Lecture 16.

Definition 17.1. Let MMM be a smooth manifold with an affine connection ∇\nabla∇. The curvature operator is defined on smooth fields by R(X,Y)Z=∇X∇YZ−∇Y∇XZ−∇[X,Y]Z.\Riemann(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z.R(X,Y)Z=∇X​∇Y​Z−∇Y​∇X​Z−∇[X,Y]​Z.

Curvature measures the leading change under transport around a small loop. 17.3 Ricci, scalar and sectional curvature

Definition 17.7. Self-check.

State in one sentence what the curvature tensor is and why it was introduced. Explain why a nonzero Christoffel symbol does not by itself establish nonzero curvature.

Answer.

The curvature tensor is the function-linear operator measuring the leading failure of parallel transport to return a vector unchanged around small coordinate loops. The polar plane has nonzero Christoffel symbols but Rrϕrϕ=−1+1=0\Riemann^r{}_{\phi r\phi}=-1+1=0Rrϕrϕ​=−1+1=0. The derivative and product terms cancel, so a connection coefficient alone does not measure curvature.

The second Bianchi identity

Curvature forms satisfy a differential identity because d2=0\dd^2=0d2=0. Lecture 19 contracts this identity to obtain a divergence-free tensor in the gravitational field equation. The curvature-form calculation used no metric hypothesis. Thus Ω=dω+ω∧ω\curvaturematrix=\dd\form+\form\wedge\formΩ=dω+ω∧ω holds in any local frame for any affine connection. Differentiate a tensor by the connection product rule: ∇XT\nabla_XT∇X​T differentiates the output and subtracts a term for the covariant derivative of each input. For curvature, this means (∇XR)(Y,Z)W=∇X(R(Y,Z)W)−R(∇XY,Z)W−R(Y,∇XZ)W−R(Y,Z)∇XW.\begin{split} (\nabla_X\Riemann)(Y,Z)W &=\nabla_X(\Riemann(Y,Z)W)-\Riemann(\nabla_XY,Z)W\\ &\quad-\Riemann(Y,\nabla_XZ)W-\Riemann(Y,Z)\nabla_XW. \end{split}(∇X​R)(Y,Z)W​=∇X​(R(Y,Z)W)−R(∇X​Y,Z)W−R(Y,∇X​Z)W−R(Y,Z)∇X​W.​

Summary

Curvature is the tensor R(X,Y)=[∇X,∇Y]−∇[X,Y]\Riemann(X,Y)=[\nabla_X,\nabla_Y]-\nabla_{[X,Y]}R(X,Y)=[∇X​,∇Y​]−∇[X,Y]​. Metric compatibility and zero torsion give its antisymmetries and first Bianchi identity. For a Riemannian metric, Ricci sums sectional curvatures through a unit direction, and scalar curvature is twice the sum over basis planes. A bi-invariant metric gives R(X,Y)Z=−14[[X,Y],Z]\Riemann(X,Y)Z=-\tfrac14[[X,Y],Z]R(X,Y)Z=−41​[[X,Y],Z] on left-invariant fields. In a local orthonormal frame, Cartan’s equations replace Christoffel computations by dθ=−ω∧θ\dd\coframe=-\form\wedge\coframedθ=−ω∧θ and Ω=dω+ω∧ω\curvaturematrix=\dd\form+\form\wedge\formΩ=dω+ω∧ω. On a surface, dω12=Kθ1∧θ2\dd\surfaceconn=\Gausscurv\coframe^1\wedge\coframe^2dω12​=Kθ1∧θ2. The sphere gives positive curvature, the half-plane negative curvature, and the flat quotient torus and cylinder zero curvature.
