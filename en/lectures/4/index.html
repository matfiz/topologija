Covering spaces and rotations

What this lecture does

We generalise the circle projection of Lecture 3 to covering maps. Their local inverse branches lift paths and homotopies. For a simply connected covering space, lifted endpoints determine loop classes. We apply this to rotations: one full turn is nontrivial, and two full turns contract.

Definition 4.3 (Lift). A lift of f ⁣:Y→Xf\colon Y\to Xf:Y→X is a continuous map f~ ⁣:Y→X~\tilde f\colon Y\to\widetilde Xf~​:Y→X with p∘f~=fp\circ\tilde f=fp∘f~​=f.

Theorem 4.4 (Path and path-homotopy lifting). For a covering ppp, a path γ ⁣:I→X\gamma\colon I\to Xγ:I→X has a unique lift after specifying γ~(0)\tilde\gamma(0)γ~​(0) above γ(0)\gamma(0)γ(0). A homotopy h ⁣:I2→Xh\colon I^2\to Xh:I2→X has a unique lift after a lift of its bottom edge has been specified. If hhh fixes its path endpoints, so does its lift.

Proof. First, uniqueness of a path lift follows locally. On a subinterval mapping into an evenly covered UUU, any lift stays in one sheet: the interval is connected and the sheets are disjoint and open. The sheet is determined by one lifted value. Its inverse branch determines the whole subinterval.

For existence, pull back evenly covered sets to an open cover of III. The Lebesgue-number lemma of Lemma 3.5 gives a finite subdivision such that each closed subinterval maps into one such set. On the first subinterval use the sheet containing the prescribed initial point. On each successive subinterval use the sheet containing the preceding endpoint. The formulas agree at the joins and paste continuously. Local uniqueness on successive subintervals gives uniqueness on III.

For a homotopy, pull the same open cover back to the compact square. Choose a grid with each cell mapping into an evenly covered set. Extend the bottom lift row by row, from left to right. Choose on each cell the inverse branch whose sheet contains the already assigned lower-left value. On its bottom and left edges, path-lift uniqueness forces agreement with every previously assigned value. There are no other previously assigned points on the cell. Finite pasting gives a continuous lift on the square. The same cell order proves uniqueness. This is the grid construction of Lemma 3.6, with sheets in place of integer translates.

Finally, if an edge of hhh is constant, its lift is a path in a discrete fibre. A continuous image of the connected interval is connected, so this path is constant. Apply this to the two endpoint edges. 

In particular, endpoint-fixed homotopic paths have lifts with the same endpoint when their initial lifts agree. The induced homomorphism p∗p_*p∗​ is injective: lift a based contraction of a projected loop. The lift is a based contraction of the original loop. Also, [γ]∈p∗π1(X~,x~0)⟺the lift of γ from x~0 closes.[\gamma]\in p_*\fundamentalgroup(\widetilde X,\tilde x_0) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the lift of $\gamma$ from $\tilde x_0$ closes}.[γ]∈p∗​π1​(X,x~0​)⟺the lift of γ from x~0​ closes. 4.3 Fibres, loop classes and deck transformations

For the circle projection, translations u↦u+ku\mapsto u+ku↦u+k with k∈Zk\in\Zk∈Z preserve the projected point. Composing translations adds their integers, just as concatenating loops adds winding numbers. We use transformations preserving a covering projection to recover the multiplication of loop classes from lifted endpoints.

Definition 4.5 (Deck transformation). A deck transformation of ppp is a homeomorphism D ⁣:X~→X~D\colon\widetilde X\to\widetilde XD:X→X with p∘D=pp\circ D=pp∘D=p. Their group under composition is written Deck⁡(p)\Deck(p)Deck(p).

On a path-connected covering space, a deck transformation is determined by its value at one point. 4.4 The antipodal cover and rotations

Proposition 4.9 (Spheres of dimension at least twosketched). The sphere Sn\sphere nSn is simply connected for n≥2n\ge2n≥2.

Proof sketch. Short spherical arcs join nonantipodal points. A path via a third point joins antipodes, so the sphere is path-connected.

A loop missing a point contracts after stereographic projection to Rn\R^nRn. An arbitrary continuous loop need not miss a point. We first replace the loop by a homotopic spherical polygon, whose image lies in finitely many proper linear subspaces.

First, cover the loop by open hemispheres. Compactness and Lemma 3.5 give a finite subdivision whose pieces each lie in one hemisphere. Replace each piece by the shortest spherical arc joining its endpoints in that hemisphere. Normalizing their straight interpolation gives the homotopy: its scalar product with the hemisphere centre stays positive.

Next, the resulting polygon lies in finitely many two-dimensional linear subspaces. For n≥2n\ge2n≥2 these are proper subspaces of Rn+1\R^{n+1}Rn+1 and their union misses a point of the sphere. Finally, stereographic projection from that point identifies its complement with Rn\R^nRn, where the polygon contracts with its base point fixed. The formulas of Lecture 1 apply in nnn coordinates. This sketches the sphere result of Hatcher, Proposition 1.14, using polygonal approximation.

This also distinguishes the plane from higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces, given Proposition 4.9 at its sketched level. Self-check.

State in one sentence what a covering map is and why we introduced it to study loop classes. Explain why specifying one starting point upstairs is essential.

Answer.

A covering map is a continuous surjection with disjoint local inverse sheets, introduced to lift paths and homotopies and to recover loop classes from their endpoints when the covering space is simply connected. The initial point selects a sheet on the first subinterval. Each successive endpoint selects the next sheet, which is the decisive step in path-lift existence and uniqueness.

Summary

Local inverse sheets lift paths and their homotopies uniquely. For a simply connected covering space, based loop classes correspond to fibre points. A transitive deck group gives the same group structure after choosing a lifted base point. The antipodal cover computes π1(RPn)≅Z2\fundamentalgroup(\RP n) \cong\Z_2π1​(RPn)≅Z2​ for n≥2n\ge2n≥2, using the sketched sphere result. In SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3), a full turn is nontrivial and a double turn is trivial. Lecture 5 computes fundamental groups by joining open pieces.
